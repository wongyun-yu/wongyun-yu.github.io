Scaling Human Demonstrations into Physically Executable Whole-Robot Data

Wongyun Yu1,2, Honggeun Kim1, Donguk Lee2, Minchan Kim2, Kyoung Whan Choe2, Minsu Cho1,2

1POSTECH  2RLWRLD

CodeData

Abstract

Many recent works retarget human hand-object demonstrations to robot hands. To train whole-robot policies, however, the retargeted motions must be kinematically feasible and physically executable, and be produced efficiently at scale.

We present DexEngine, a simulation pipeline that turns human manipulation data into train-ready, physically executable arm-hand trajectories. DexEngine first places each clip’s hand-object motion where the arms can reach it, finds an arm motion that follows the human hands, and then corrects the motion in physics simulation with residual reinforcement learning.

From nine human hand-object datasets, we generate about 5,200 clips for each of four dexterous hands, augmented into 65,000 trajectories (116 h) and 325,000 episodes (578 h) in total. Even with arms attached, DexEngine tracks objects more accurately than prior floating-hand methods and runs over 10× faster than RL-based ones. Policies trained on our data far outperform those trained on the same clips without physics correction or with the arms attached after a floating-hand correction, showing that fine-tuning data need physics correction. Mid-training on our data further improves them at equal fine-tuning budget, in simulation and on a real robot.

Limitations of retargeted data

As training data for a whole robot, retargeted data have three limitations.

  1. Physics

    Kinematic methods ignore dynamics. Without contact and force, the robot may fail to move the object.

  2. Arms

    Most physics-based methods drive floating hands. They leave the arm’s joint limits, reach and collisions unchecked.

  3. Scale

    Physics-based methods do not scale. The few that include the arm cover fewer than a thousand human demonstrations.

Pipeline

DexEngine satisfies the kinematic constraint before physics, and then reduces the object error in simulation.

Kinematic reference generation

We search each clip’s scene placements for one that the arms can follow.

Placement search, arm IK and retiming

A placement is a translation and a yaw of the demonstrated hands and objects relative to the robot base. A cheap screening reads a reachability map, precomputed per robot hand and arm, and discards most candidates. A candidate survives if at least 80% of its frames are covered for each active arm. The arm IK then solves every frame of the survivors.

IK solved at each frame alone can switch branches and make the arm jump. Dynamic programming over sparse covered frames picks one branch for the whole clip, the coarse path. Including the coarse path among the 32 IK starts raises the share of kept clips from 70% to 89% among 64 clips. Retiming then stretches each interval until its fastest joint stays slightly below its velocity limit.

Physics correction with residual RL

The policy acts on the wrist pose. Each random start resets the arm to the kinematic reference instead of re-solving IK.

Arm attachment in residual RL

One policy corrects a bundle of up to 64 clips. It outputs residuals for the wrist targets and finger joints, and differential IK moves the arms. On the 64 clips of the pipeline comparison, resetting the arm to our reference raises ADD-AUC from 0.544 to 0.856 with residuals on the arm joints, and from 0.832 to 0.900 with residuals on the wrist, the highest. With wrist residuals, re-solving the arm scores even below training without random starts (0.888). Random starts help only when they place the arm on one consistent motion.

Our placement search keeps a kinematic reference for 43.8% of 128 sampled clips, against 1.6% at the default scene position. Testing every candidate keeps 45.3% but takes three times longer. A single IK run from the rest pose keeps only 28% of 64 clips within the arm limits, against 89% for our arm IK. Training with floating hands and attaching the arm later, even at our placements, passes 26% fewer clips through our arm IK.
